The Psychology of Learning

Learning shapes who we are, but is not the same for everyone. No two brains are alike. Dive into the concept of learning now!

What is Learning?

In psychology, learning is the process of acquiring new information through experience or behaviors. Because of learning, we humans are able to adapt to our environments.

"Learning is not simply storing information; it is changing the way we understand and interact with the world."
Key Idea: Learning involves experience, practice, rerouting, and adaptation. It is one of the most important processes in which is studied in psychology.

Defining Conditioning

Conditioning is the process by which we learn associations. There are two main types: classical conditioning and operant conditioning.

In classical conditioning, an association between two stimuli is learned to anticipate events. "We learn that a flash of lightning signals an impending crack of thunder, so when lightning flashes nearby, we start to brace ourselves," Myers, D. G. (2008).

In operant conditioning, we learn to associate a behavior with its consequence. When a reward follows a behavior or action, we are more likely to repeat it; when a punishment follows a behavior or action, we often avoid repeating it.

Types of Learning

Classical Conditioning

A type of learning in which one learns to associate two or more stimuli and thus anticipate events. - A neutral stimulus (NS), a stimulus that elicits no response prior to conditioning, becomes linked with a meaningful stimulus.

Operant Conditioning

A type of learning in which behavior is strengthened if followed by a reinforcer or diminished if followed by a punishment.

Observational Learning

Learning initiated by observing others. We humans have the tendency to imitate behaviors observed within people around us.

Classical Conditioning

Understanding classical conditioning through Ivan Pavlov, a Russian-Soviet neurologist and physiologist whose early twentieth-century experiment on classical conditioning remains foundational to psychology. Pavlov's work also aided many psychologists, including being foundational to John B. Watson's ideas. Watson and Pavlov both shared an indifference to "mentalistic" concepts and believed that the basic laws of learning were mutual for all animals.

Pavlov observed that putting food in a dog's mouth caused it to salivate. The dog's salivation not only began in response to the taste of the food, but also began when the food was in mere sight of the dog, as well as in response to the person delivering the food, or the sound of the person's approaching footsteps. Pavlov considered these secretions a distraction until he realized they pointed to a simple, but important, form of learning.

Pavlov's Experiment

To explore this phenomenon objectively, Pavlov and his team isolated the dog in a small room, secured it using a harness, and attached a device to redirect saliva into a measuring instrument. They presented food by sliding a food bowl in and blowing meat powder into the dog's mouth at a precise moment.

Just before placing food in the dog's mouth, Pavlov sounded a tone. After several pairings of tone and food, the dog — anticipating the meat powder — began salivating to the tone alone. In later experiments, a buzzer, a light, and a touch on the leg were also used as signals.

Before Conditioning: Tone → No Salivation
During Conditioning: Tone + Food → Salivation
After Conditioning: Tone → Salivation

Few researchers today propose that psychology should ignore mental processes, however a large number of researchers agree that classical conditioning is a fundamental form of learning that enables all living organisms to adapt to their environment.

Operant Conditioning

In operant conditioning, association between a behavior and its consequence are learnt. Psychologist B.F. Skinner proposed that behavior is shaped by repercussions. Positive reinforcement promotes behavior by adding something pleasant, while punishment decreases behavior.

Positive Reinforcement

Receiving praise for completing homework.

Negative Reinforcement

Stopping an annoying alarm by getting out of bed.

Punishment

Losing privileges after breaking rules.

Observational Learning

Canadian-American psychologist, Albert Bandura, indicated that people learn by observing others. Children often tend to imitate behaviors they observe from parents, family members, peers, teachers, etc.

Observation → Attention → Memory → Imitation

Learning Through Life

Childhood

Language, social skills, and basic behaviors develop.

Adolescence

Complex reasoning and identity formation emerge.

Adulthood

Learning continues through careers, hobbies, and relationships.

Why Learning Matters

Learning allows for us humans to adapt to new environments, problem solve using critical thinking, communicate effectively, and build functioning societies. Understanding how learning works and how crucial it is in our everyday lives helps us to become more effective lifelong learners.
